# Voice

Derived from what Asmit actually wrote — the Torii and Lead Engine write-ups,
his portfolio framing, his LinkedIn posts. Not invented.

## The positioning: someone who asks the right questions

This is the throughline for every cover letter.

**The rule that makes or breaks it: never claim it. Demonstrate it.**

"I'm someone who asks the right questions" is a sentence anyone can type, which
is exactly why it reads as filler — and it is the kind of self-characterization
that gets a letter flagged as AI-written. A reader decides you ask good questions
by watching you ask one. So a letter opens with the question, not the claim.

The claim is also unusually easy for him to back, because his projects *are*
questions made concrete:

| Project | The question he asked that others weren't |
|---|---|
| **Torii** | "When an autonomous agent spends money, who is accountable?" Everyone was building agents that could pay for things. He asked who was on the hook when one overspends — and built the boundary. |
| **Lead Engine** | "Why did the model score this lead an 83?" He made every score carry written reasoning and every pipeline stage visible. His words: *no black boxes*. |
| **MyYogaTeacher** | "What is the admin team actually doing all day?" The answer was Ctrl+F and edit. He replaced the real workflow, not the specified one. |
| **WatchDNA** | "How does this dataset stay correct next month?" Not "how do I load it once" — so he built endpoint discovery instead of a scraper run. |
| **AI Society** | "What does someone with no background actually need to hear first?" That is a teaching question, and it is why the workshops worked. |

His own sentence, which is the thesis in miniature:

> The human controls policy. The agent controls execution.
> Torii is the boundary between them.

That is what asking the right question sounds like. Use it.

## How a cover letter carries this

**Opening (his, always — the system will not write this).** Name the question
their work raises for him. It must be specific enough that it could not be asked
of a competitor, and honest enough that he actually wants the answer. Not
flattery in interrogative form ("How does your team achieve such innovation?").
A real question, the kind you would ask in the first ten minutes on the job.

**Middle (system-assembled).** One or two things he built that came from asking
that same class of question. Facts from profile.yaml, verbatim. This is where the
claim gets earned — the reader sees the pattern rather than being told about it.

**Close (plain).** What he wants and what is true about his availability. No
ceremony.

If the letter's question could be pasted into a letter to any other employer, it
is not a question, it is a compliment. Cut it and start again.

## Register

- **Declarative.** He states what a thing does. He does not sell it.
- **Concrete nouns over abstractions.** "SQLite audit ledger", "county records",
  "Ctrl+F and edit" — not "robust data infrastructure".
- **Constraints stated plainly, as a point of pride.** "Two production
  dependencies." "Built the full stack in a day." "~700 lines." He treats a
  small number as the interesting fact, which is a real engineer's instinct.
- **Mechanism before outcome.** He explains how it works, then what it did. Most
  resumes do the reverse and sound hollow for it.
- **Occasional image, used once and not explained.** "A walkable computer."
  "The boundary between them." One per document, maximum.
- **No hedging, no enthusiasm words.** He never writes "excited", "passionate",
  "thrilled". Do not introduce them.

## Forbidden here specifically

Beyond the standing lint rules in `engine/rules_ai_tells.yaml`:

- Any sentence claiming he asks good questions, thinks deeply, is curious, or is
  a problem-solver. Show it or drop it.
- Rhetorical questions used as transitions ("So what does that mean for your
  team?"). A question in his letter is a real one, asked once, at the top.
- Explaining the metaphor after using it.
- Restating the job description back at them.
