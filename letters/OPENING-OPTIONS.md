# The personal paragraph — options

**No quote. No name.** Naming a philosopher turns a point of view into a
citation, and the reader stops hearing you and starts grading your reading list.
The ideology is the *shape* of the paragraph: someone who knows it recognises
it, someone who does not just reads a person who thinks clearly. That is the
version that is actually hard to imitate.

Rules all of these follow:

1. The ideology never appears by name.
2. It explains a real thing you built. Philosophy attached to nothing is a
   horoscope.
3. You never state the trait. Not "I am curious", not "I ask the right
   questions." You ask one, and the reader concludes it.
4. One idea per paragraph.

---

# SOCRATIC — the method is admitted ignorance

The Socratic move is to profess *not knowing*, and then refuse to move on until
the thing is understood. Humble on the surface, relentless underneath. Reads as
likeable and rigorous at once, and it is the lower-risk of the two.

## Option 1 — "I could not explain it to myself"

I am not the strongest engineer in most rooms I have been in. What I do have is
a low tolerance for not understanding something, and no talent for pretending
otherwise. Everyone building agents this year accepts that an agent can hold a
wallet and spend from it. I could not explain to myself who was accountable when
one overspent, and rather than assume somebody smarter had already settled it, I
stayed with the question until I could build the answer. Torii is that answer: a
proxy between an agent and every paid tool it calls, where the human owns the
policy, the agent owns execution, and every blocked call is written down with
its reason. Most of what I have built started as something I could not explain
to myself.

*Strongest opening line of the set. The admission buys enormous credibility for
everything after it, and the last sentence reframes the whole paragraph as a
method rather than a mood.*

## Option 2 — the question that annoys the room

The most useful thing I do on a team is ask the question everyone has stopped
asking. It is not always welcome. At MyYogaTeacher I was asked to improve an
admin tool, and before touching it I spent a while watching what the team
actually did all day, which turned out to be Ctrl+F and edit, over and over.
The tool was not the problem. The workflow was, so I replaced it with a
voice-to-SQL pipeline and the tool stopped mattering. I have found that a brief
is somebody's description of a problem, and the description is worth checking
before the code is.

*The most concrete, and the funniest. "It is not always welcome" is the line a
hiring manager remembers, because it is honest about a cost.*

# NIETZSCHEAN — the values are made, not inherited

The Nietzschean move is genealogical: every settled thing was decided by
somebody once, for reasons that may have expired. Bolder, more assertive, more
distinctive — and slightly riskier, because conviction without warmth can read
as arrogance. These are written to keep the warmth.

## Option 3 — who decided this, and does it still hold

Every system I have worked in carries at least one decision that somebody made
once and nobody has revisited since. It stops looking like a decision and starts
looking like the weather. The part of engineering I like most is finding those
and asking whether they still hold. Agents holding wallets is one: the industry
settled it early, and I could not find anyone who had asked what happens when
one overspends. So I built the boundary I wanted to exist — Torii, where a human
owns the policy and the agent only owns execution, and the proxy reserves the
money before the call rather than apologising after it. The assumption nobody
argues about any more is usually where the thing breaks.

*The most intellectually distinctive. "It stops looking like a decision and
starts looking like the weather" is the best sentence in this document.*

## Option 4 — I do not inherit the brief

I have a hard time treating a brief as the problem. A brief is one person's
description of the problem, written before anyone looked, and I would rather go
and look. That instinct is why Torii exists at all — nobody asked for a spending
proxy for AI agents, and I built one because the alternative was accepting that
an autonomous system should be able to spend money with nobody accountable for
it. It is also why the Lead Engine writes out its reasoning next to every score
it assigns: a number you cannot interrogate is not a number, it is a rumour.

*The most confident, and the most "founder". "A rumour" is a genuinely good
line. Use where initiative is valued over deference — startups, research, small
teams. Not for a bank.*

# Choosing

| | tone | risk | best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** Could not explain it | humble, rigorous | lowest | anywhere; large formal employers |
| **2** The annoying question | dry, concrete | low | teams that value product sense |
| **3** The weather | intellectual, assured | medium | research, infra, AI labs |
| **4** Not the brief | confident, founder | higher | startups, small teams |

If picking one: **1 for reach, 3 for the roles you actually want.**

# The two signposts

**Highlighted first line:**

1. The paragraph below is the one I would want you to read. The one after it is
   the one you are paid to read.
2. Next paragraph: who I am. The one after that: why I can do the work. Read
   them in whichever order you trust.
3. What follows is the part that does not fit on a resume. The technical case
   starts one paragraph down.

**Second signpost:**

- If you would rather have evidence than philosophy, this is where it starts.
- Here is the part you can check.
- The technical case, briefly.

Note that signpost 1 and "philosophy" in the second one now name a thing the
letter never does explicitly. Consider *"If you would rather have evidence than
opinion, this is where it starts"* — it keeps the promise without labelling the
paragraph above as philosophy.

# CHOSEN — Option 1, developed

The version now in the draft. Three things had to happen at once: the admission
has to *buy* credibility rather than spend it, the humility has to be earned
rather than announced, and it has to land somewhere useful to someone deciding
whether to hire an intern.

> I am not the best engineer in most rooms I have been in, and I have no talent
> for pretending otherwise. What I do instead is say when I have lost the
> thread, and then go and find the person who has it. Teaching machine learning
> for a year to students with no background turned that into a habit rather than
> an embarrassment: you cannot explain something badly to four people and still
> believe you understood it. It is also where the work starts. I could not
> explain to myself who is accountable when an AI agent overspends, so I stayed
> with the question until I could build an answer — Torii, where a human owns
> the policy, the agent owns execution, and every blocked call is written down
> with its reason. I have a great deal left to learn, which is most of why I
> want to spend a summer as the least experienced person on a good team.

**The teaching sentence is the hinge.** Mentoring four freshmen is a credential
that looks like the *opposite* of humility. Using it as the *source* of the
humility is what stops the whole paragraph reading as false modesty — you are
not claiming to be modest, you are describing what teaching did to you.

**The last line is doing the most work.** "The least experienced person on a
good team" converts humility into a reason to hire you. Coachability is close to
the top of what interns are screened on, and this claims it without ever using
the word.

**Do not add a second admission.** One is credibility; two is a pattern, and the
reader starts believing you.

## Swappable lines

**If "not the best engineer" feels too strong:**
> I am rarely the most experienced person in the room I am working in, and I
> have no talent for pretending otherwise.

**If the teaching sentence should hit harder:**
> …you find out exactly which parts you only thought you understood.

**Alternative closes:**
- …which is most of why I want a summer somewhere the people around me are
  better than I am.
- …and the fastest way I know to fix that is to be in a room where I am the one
  asking the questions.
